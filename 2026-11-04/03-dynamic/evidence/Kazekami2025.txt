ABSTRACT
Using job advertisement data in Japan, we examine the effects of minimum wage (MW) changes 
on labour demand and how regional differences influence the impact of these changes. Previous 
research has struggled to fully evaluate the consequences of MW changes because of varying local 
labour–market conditions and methodological challenges. This study focuses on the demand side 
by analysing changes in job offers, which better reflect firms’ hiring intentions independent of 
supply shifts. Our findings reveal that MW increases lead to a net increase in job listings, with a rise 
in jobs above the new MW exceeding the decline in jobs below it, resulting in a total increase of 
approximately 10–16%. The positive response is more pronounced in low-density, monopsonistic 
regions, while high-density competitive markets tend to show a reduction in job offers. The 
negative impacts are particularly noticeable in clerical, manufacturing, logistics, and cleaning 
jobs, where competition is high. However, professional jobs show a minimal impact. Our results 
highlight that the effects of MW increases depend on regional labour–market structures and the 
competitive dynamics of specific occupations – policymakers should explicitly account for such 
heterogeneity when designing MW policies.

I. Introduction
We used unique data from a Japanese job advertising 
Company A to analyse the impact of minimum wages 
(MWs) on firms’ job offers. The existing literature has 
primarily focused on the impact of MWs changes on 
the number of workers employed by firms. However, 
we argue that in a labour market such as Japan, where 
dismissals because of MW increases are not permitted 
and employment is relatively stable, the impact of 
MWs manifests more strongly in job offers rather 
than in employment levels. To the best of our knowl­
edge, except for Kudlyak, Tasci, and Tüzemen (2022), 
few studies have examined the effects of MWs on job 
offers as we have done, and our research contributes 
to the existing literature in this area. Furthermore, we 
examine how the effect of MWs on the number of job 
offers differs depending on the state of competition 
for applicants, which varies across labour markets.
The effect of MWs legislation on employment has 
been the subject of considerable debate in numerous 
countries – from an academic standpoint and in the 
context of economic and social policy. Although the 
MW system is a significant social issue, previous 
studies have yielded inconclusive results. Some stu­
dies have indicated a negative impact on employ­
ment, while others have not found such an effect.
In a perfectly competitive labour market, an 
increase in the MWs above the equilibrium wage 
would result in a decline in labour demand and an 
increase in labour supply. In this case, an increase in 
the MWs may result in higher wages for some work­
ers. Nevertheless, this will not lead to an improve­
ment in overall welfare because it will also result in 
job losses. Neumark and Shirley (2022) reported that 
most (79.2%) of the estimates included in studies 
that utilized their established criteria indicate 
a negative value of MW employment elasticity – 
raising MWs would likely eliminate jobs and reduce 
economic welfare. Furthermore, roughly half of 
these estimates were found to be statistically signifi­
cant. The researchers observed that the influence of 
MWs on younger workers and those with less edu­
cation is especially pronounced – an increase in 
MWs will result in a reduction in their employment.

Nevertheless, Manning (2021) highlights that 
the conclusion that MWs have a negative impact 
on employment is not straightforward, citing sev­
eral theoretical and analytical challenges. This 
study examines one such issue – the various pro­
blems inherent in a non-competitive labour market 
with friction. In the context of an imperfectly com­
petitive labour market, Manning (2021) posits that 
the impact of a MW increase on employment 
remains a priori unknown. Card and Krueger 
(1995) and Manning (2003) conclude that imper­
fectly competitive labour markets do not necessa­
rily reduce labour demand, and if this is the case, 
MWs could contribute to improved welfare levels.
From the perspective of imperfectly competitive 
markets, the respective reactions of labour supply 
and demand to MWs must be differentiated. 
Portugal and Rute Cardoso (2006), Brochu and 
Green (2013), and Dube, William Lester, and Reich 
(2016) analyse the impact of MWs on labour flows 
rather than on the size of the employees. Although 
they consider changes in employer and worker beha­
viour to some extent by distinguishing between leav­
ing employment (outflow) and entering employment 
(inflow), they could not capture employer and worker 
behaviour separately. While we cannot observe the 
response of workers, we can consider the impact of 
MWs on employers by observing the number of job 
offers. If MWs reduce the number of job offers, even if 
labour supply increases, it should have a negative 
impact on the volume of labour demand. Thus, we 
consider the number of job offers to be a more sig­
nificant indicator of the employment market than the 
volume of employment.1
The data utilized in this study, provided by job 
advertising Company A, allow for categorizing the 
number of jobs by hourly wage range. This enables 
an examination of the impact of changes in the MWs 
on specific wage ranges2; how did the number of job 
openings near the minimum wage and the number of 
high-paying job openings change after the minimum 
wage was raised? From the same perspective as our 
research, Cengiz et al. (2019) analysed the impact of 
the MWs by focusing on changes in the wage 
distribution rather than total employment levels. 
They created bins by dividing the wage distribution 
for each period into increments of $0.25 and used 
Difference-in-Difference (DID) to estimate how the 
employment contained in each bin changed over time 
before and after the MW change. According to their 
analysis, employment levels declined significantly 
among workers whose wages fell below the MWs 
while exhibiting an increase in employment for 
those whose wages were slightly above the MWs. 
However, the influence is constrained to a specific 
wage range, with no discernible employment shifts 
observed among those at the upper end of the wage 
distribution. In the following analysis, we also aggre­
gate job offers by wage bin, similar to them, and 
estimate the increase or decrease in job offers for 
each wage bin before and after the MWs change 
using DID. This allows us to observe which wage 
levels’ job offers are affected and how long the impact 
persists.
Furthermore, as the competitive state of the 
labour market varies by region, we attempt to 
examine how the effects of the MWs differ 
across regions. Our data can be analysed by 
region, enabling an examination of the discre­
pancies in the impact of MWs across disparate 
labour–market 
conditions 
and 
regions. 
Significant regional differences exist in wage 
levels, and part of this is attributable to regional 
variations in the characteristics of the labour 
force. However, the offering wages used in this 
study are for part-time workers. Therefore, 
worker characteristics have minimum influence 
on 
regional 
differences 
in 
wage 
levels. 
Nonetheless, regional labour–market character­
istics may exert an influence on the impact of 
changes in the MW. In a competitive labour 
market with numerous recruiting firms and job 
seekers, an increase in MWs may potentially 
lead to a reduction in the number of job offers. 
As an alternative possibility, if the labour mar­
ket is characterized by a tight supply–demand 
balance or if firms have a significant number of 
unfilled positions, they may attempt to increase

Preliminary estimates suggest that the num­

increase following a MW change, while that 
below the MWs will decline, resulting in an

Concurrently, evidence suggests that increases 
in the MWs result in a concomitant rise in 
jobs at higher wage levels. However, this effect 
is mitigated at the higher end of the wage dis­

for applicants. Conversely, such changes have 
been found to result in an increase in employ­

in a decline in the number of job openings in 
occupations that face significant competition for 
applicants. Consequently, our estimates corro­
borate the theoretical prediction that an increase 
in the MWs leads to a decrease in job openings 
in competitive markets and an increase in job 
openings in monopsonic markets.

Section II elaborates the details of the data, meth­

II. Data and empirical models
Data
In this study, we utilize job advertisement data pro­
vided 
by 
Japanese 
Company 
A. 
The 
job 
advertisements published by Company A encompass 
part- and full-time positions; however, the data we 
employ are exclusively for part-time positions. Most 
extant studies have focused on analysing minimum 
wage-sensitive workers (see Allegretto et al. 2017; 
Card 1992a, 1992b; Card and Krueger, 1994; Dube 
et al. 2010; Katz and Krueger 1992; Neumark and 
Wascher 1992; Neumark et al. 2014). Our analysis 
aligns with their approaches. A secondary rationale 
for constraining our analysis to part-time employ­
ment is that all recruiters specify the advertised wage 
for part-time positions as an hourly wage. Moreover, 
the posted wage is typically implemented at the time 
of hire.
Our dataset comprises information on approxi­
mately 28.26 million job advertisements from 
October 2018 to February 2024 across the three 
major economic zones: the Tokyo, Kansai, and 
Chukyo metropolitan areas. Revisions occur each 
October, as described in detail in the next section. 
Accordingly, we focus on annual changes in the 
MWs from 2019 to 2023. Notably, during the 2020 
revision, when the pandemic was at its peak, there 
was almost no change in the MWs. The dataset 
includes the publication date, the location of the 
recruiting establishment, industry, occupation, and 
the recruiting wage.
Figure 1 compares our dataset from Company 
A and the contemporaneous number of new vacan­
cies of part-time jobs posted on the Public 
Employment Security Offices across the country. 
This assesses the data’s alignment with Japanese 
labour market trends. The trend itself remains rela­
tively consistent until 2019, although there are 
some notable discrepancies. Company A’s postings 
were slightly below public‑agency listings and 
exhibited month‑to‑month volatility. To mitigate 
this, our empirical analysis compares two‑month 
windows before and after each MW revision rather 
than single months. Both sources saw a sharp 
decline in 2020 because of the COVID-19 pan­
demic; however, from 2021 onward, Company A’s 
postings have grown much faster and now exceed 
those of public agencies. Company A’s data serve as 
a reliable proxy for total Japanese job offers and are 
being used by the government to publish more 
timely wage trend statistics (Nikkei Shinbun, 
a 
major 
Japanese 
economics 
newspaper, 
6 April 2021).

Japanese MW system
Table 1 shows the MWs of each prefecture from 
2018 to 2023. In Japan, the MW is determined by 
the Minimum Wage Act, and employers must pay 
employees an amount greater than the MW. There 
are two types of MWs: the regional MW, applicable 
to all employees and employers living in a specific 
prefecture, and the specific MW for employers and 
employees in specific industries. Regional and spe­
cific MWs are modified simultaneously, and the 
higher one is enforced. This study uses the regional 
MW because this type of MW is greater than the 
specific MW in the three major economic zones 
that we investigated. This system contrasts with 
that of the US, where states and localities can set 
their MWs higher than the federal standard.

Effect of MW changes on the number of job offers: 
a conceptual framework
To clarify the identification strategy used in our 
empirical analysis, we first provide a conceptual 
explanation of how changes in MWs may affect job 
offers. The following discussion outlines the key idea 
behind our approach using the structure of Japan’s 
MW system and the heterogeneity in wage distribu­
tions, particularly the differences between jobs close 
to the MW and those further above it.
In accordance with the Japanese MW system, 
changes in MWs are implemented concurrently 
throughout the country, although the magnitude 
of the increase differs across regions. Thus, 
a comparison between regions cannot be made 
where there has been a change in the MWs and 
regions where there has been no change. However, 
as our data provide information on recruiting 
wages for jobs on a precise yen-by-yen basis, we 
can compare the impact of jobs situated close to the 
MWs with those situated further away. By employ­
ing these disparities in wage levels, we can ascertain 
the influence of the MW on the number of open­
ings at each wage range by utilizing the DID esti­
mation methodology proposed by Cengiz et al. 
(2019).
Figure 4 is a theoretical graph showing the effect 
of a change in MWs on the number of job offers. The 
hypothetical frequency distribution of wages offered 
before and after the change in MWs is shown by the 
red and blue dashed lines in this figure. When the 
changed MWs is set to MW in the figure, all job 
offers in the red-dashed area should disappear if the 
labour market is perfectly competitive, as previously 
legal jobs in this area have become illegal. 
Conversely, if the labour market is a monopsony, 
jobs where marginal productivity exceeds the MW 
may shift from the red-dashed area to the blue- 
dashed area. In this case, the impact of MW on the 
quantity of jobs can be calculated as the sum of the 
jobs that remain because of marginal productivity

In such a case, a shift in the MW may not impact

diminish the substitutability between high- and

of our analysis is limited to data on part-time 
employment, job seekers and employers are unli­

occupations. Nevertheless, we also examine the 
extent to which MW effects affect wage levels.
Furthermore, we are interested in evaluating 
whether regional labour–market conditions influence 
the impact of a change in MWs. Competitive condi­
tions in the labour market may exhibit regional var­
iation, which could give rise to disparate MW 
impacts. Competitive conditions may be more severe 
in regions with a greater number of firms and work­
ers. Consequently, an increase in MW may result in 
a reduction in the job offer rate. Conversely, in a tight 
labour market or one with numerous unfilled vacan­
cies, firms may respond to an increase in MWs by 
raising wages to incentivize job seekers. Therefore, the 
size of a region’s population and the accumulation of 
human capital may influence the availability of MWs 
and the volume of job offers. Additionally, we look at 
differences in the effect of MWs by job type, as we 
expect competition to vary by job type even within 
the same region; firms’ response to an MW increase 
and job seekers’ entry behaviours are likely to differ 
by the nature of the job type.

Exploring the regional differences

wages when the MW increases. Conversely, in 
the high-population density region, the local 
labour market is competitive, job offers below 
the new MW disappear, and the number of job

IV. Discussions and conclusions
We consider the impact of MW on labour demand 
by observing the number of job listings. A growing 
body of research on changes in MW has been 
conducted. Nonetheless, the impact of MW is elu­
sive because it depends on labour–market condi­
tions and has various theoretical and analytical 
difficulties. Therefore, we treat the number of job 
listings as a superior indicator of labour demand, as 
it captures employers’ hiring intentions indepen­
dently of supply shifts, enabling a clear distinction 
between demand- and supply-side responses to 
MW adjustments. Simultaneously, this study ana­
lyzes how different local labour–market conditions 
affect the impact of a change in the MW. Previous 
studies divide the regions based on how high or low 
the MW is and do not investigate the factors for 
disparities. Additionally, based on prior findings, 
while a MW change affects employment with 
wages close to and above the MW, regional differ­
ences have not been examined.
First, the increase in the number of jobs offered 
above the MW by 50 yen exceeds the decrease 
below the MW by 50 yen. Total changes are 
approximately 10–16% of monthly number of job 
offers in the prior estimation period. The change in 
the MW influences the upper wage ranges; how­
ever, the influence diminishes as the wage range 
increases. Second, we use population density as 
a measure of competitiveness in the local labour 
market. We empirically demonstrated that the 
high-population density and highly competitive 
local 
labour 
market 
decrease 
job 
offers. 
Conversely, 
low-population 
density 
and 
a monopsony labour market increase job offers 
when the MW increases. Consequently, company 
such as chain stores are the sole place of work in 
low-population density regions, and the wage level 
may stick to the MW. Finally, we examine the effect 
of the MW rise by occupation. As expected, in 
occupations where competition is likely to be 
intense, the following theoretical prediction holds: 
‘In competitive markets, raising the MW reduces 
job offers, while in monopsony markets, it 
increases them’.
Labour–market heterogeneity is not unique to 
Japan. Numerous studies have documented mono­
psony power; consequently, developed economies 
are characterized by labour–market heterogeneity. 
Therefore, the insights from this study have rele­
vance – and offer guidance – for policymakers and 
researchers in other advanced countries.
This study has some limitations. As our data­
set does not include information on the recruit­
ing company, even if the amount of recruitment 
remains unchanged when the MW changes, we 
cannot determine whether the reaction resulting 
from the same company or the observation 
through 
the 
recruiting 
company 
are 
exchanged.5 Additionally, with more informa­
tion about the company, we can analyse why 
the changes in the MW affect the wages of 
those with pay already above the MW or 
whether the decrease in the effect of the change 
over time is a part of the adjustment process. 
Moreover, this study does not analyse the long- 
term effects, considering the change in the sup­
ply behaviour of individuals, including migra­
tion. Although we demonstrated the different 
impacts of MWs across occupations, a more 
detailed analysis of the supply side would also 
enable us to examine how the impact of MWs 
differs by gender, as occupational choices often

In Japan, the current administration has com­
mitted to increasing the MWs to at least ¥1,500 by 
2030, which is equivalent to a 7% annual increase. 
Since the end of the pandemic in 2021, the MWs 
has increased annually by 3.7%. Leaving aside the 
debate over what the optimal level should be, 
according to our findings, even if the MWs 
increase, the likelihood of a net decrease in job 
offers is low. Instead, the effect of an average 
increase in wages offered in job offers is significant. 
However, owing to the heterogeneity of labour 
markets across regions, the effects of MWs 
increases may vary. Our findings suggest that pol­
icymakers should place greater emphasis on 
labour–market heterogeneity when setting regional 
MWs, rather than seeking to avoid being desig­
nated as the lowest MW region nationwide or to 
prevent setting a wage lower than that of neigh­
bouring regions, and rather than responding to 
governors’ concerns about their public image 
among local residents.
